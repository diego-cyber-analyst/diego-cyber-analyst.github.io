In my very first week, I realized some things would be harder than I expected. One in particular: the language.

I had studied English before — a few times, actually. But I'll be honest: spending hours with a book in my hands was never really my thing. That's exactly why I always liked IT. Most of what I know, I learned hands-on — by doing, breaking, fixing. Studying theory sitting still never worked for me the way practice does.

English was no different — except that, back in Brazil, "hands-on" English is rare. I had a decent base. I worked at a large company that had been acquired by an American multinational, so meetings with Americans and emails in English were part of my routine.

The problem is that a quick meeting, or an email, is nothing like living fully immersed in the language.

And that's exactly what Canada taught me right away.

Simple things suddenly got a little harder. Going to the grocery store to buy anything, for example. There came the doubt: the label is different, the images are different, I actually had to read to understand what it was. And what did I find? Words I had never seen before.

Makes sense, right? Like I said, my English was meeting English. And it's not hard to see that, in a war room, in the middle of an incident investigation, nobody is discussing the ingredients of some random canned food. My work vocabulary wasn't saving me in the grocery aisle.

But that, I'll admit, was actually the easy part. Phone in hand, ChatGPT open, I'd ask, translate, learn. And that's how it went — one new food at a time, one new word at a time.

The real challenge came later. Guess what the second wall was? Accents.

It wasn't my first time in Canada — but before, I'd come as a tourist, and tourism is a whole different thing. Living here, it hit me: the thing this country has the least of is Canadians. Europeans from everywhere, Indians, Africans, Iranians, Filipinos... my goodness, so many nationalities. And with them, so many levels of English, so many different accents, all at once.

I learned two things almost immediately.

The first: understanding people was going to be much harder than I imagined — not because of my English, but because everyone brought their own.

The second, and this one I carry to this day: we Brazilians worry way too much about our level of English. And the truth is, nobody — absolutely nobody — cares about it. In a country built by immigrants, everyone has an accent, everyone makes mistakes, and life goes on. The accent isn't the problem we imagine it to be.

And that's when I made a decision that, for someone who always ran from books, was almost ironic: I needed to study English again. Even the basics. Especially the basics.

Not to pass any test. But to handle real life — to learn the words I was missing, to understand the nuances I had only just started noticing now that I was living immersed in them. Practice had shown me, the hard way, exactly where I needed to go back to.

So I started. From the very beginning. From the verb *to be*. No shame, no rush — just starting over, like so many other things in my life at that moment.

And that's what I want to share with you from here on: the basics I revisited, the ones that make all the difference for anyone just arriving. Let's go.

## Survival English: the verb *to be*

If there's one place to start over, it's here. *To be* is the most basic and most used verb in English. It works as both "to exist" and "to be in a state or place," and it's the first step to building almost any sentence.

**The three forms in the present:**

| Pronoun | *to be* | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | am | I am Brazilian. | identity / who you are |
| You / We / They | are | You are late. | state / condition |
| He / She / It | is | She is at SAIT. | location / place |

One thing to know: unlike many languages that split this into two separate verbs, English uses *to be* for both who you are and how/where you are. "I am a security analyst" and "I am tired" use the exact same verb.

**Contractions (everyone uses these in real life):**

Nobody says "I am" all the time out on the street. In practice it becomes:

- I am → **I'm** (I'm from Brazil.)
- You are → **You're** (You're right.)
- He is → **He's** / She is → **She's** (She's my wife.)
- It is → **It's** (It's cold today.) ← you'll use this one A LOT in Canada
- We are → **We're** (We're new here.)
- They are → **They're** (They're from India.)

**Negative** — just add *not*:

- I'm **not** ready.
- It's **not** that cold.

**Questions** — flip the order:

- **Are** you okay?
- **Is** it far?
- **Am** I late?

In real life, right after arriving, you'll hear and use things like:

- "I'm looking for work."
- "It's my first week here."
- "Are you from around here?"
- "We're new in Calgary."

> **Tip from someone who's been there:** don't be afraid to mix up *is/are/am*. Even if you get it wrong, people understand. What matters is speaking. Remember: nobody is judging your English as much as you think.
