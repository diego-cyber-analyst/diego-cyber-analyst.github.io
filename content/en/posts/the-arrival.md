Before any suitcase, before the plane, before the Canadian cold, there was a different kind of cold — the knot in my stomach on the day I quit my job.

I had a stable job. Good salary, investments, money saved. On paper, everything people tell you not to walk away from. The severance was actually decent — even after losing part of it for resigning — and for a moment I felt a flash of joy. But it came bundled with the math I was already running in my head: that money was going to shrink, and I had nothing lined up on the other side.

I quit a week or two before the move. And they were the fullest weeks of my life. While I packed suitcases and tried to fit an entire life into a few kilos, I was also trying to be there for my family, who were going through a hard time. Starting over never comes at a clean moment. It comes in the middle of everything.

The knot in my stomach only got tighter.

On the day of the trip, we went to the airport with family. We talked, we put off the goodbye as long as we could. Then came the last hug. And then boarding.

The flight was calm. I talked with my wife until sleep got the better of her, and I sat there with my thoughts and the hum of the plane, crossing the ocean toward a life I still had no idea how to picture.

Our first landing was in Montreal — that's where we'd clear immigration before continuing on to Calgary. When the plane touched the ground, we both looked out the window at the same time.

Snow.

It was the first time either of us had ever seen snow. Everything was white, as far as we could see. I remember it like it was yesterday: my wife turned to me and said — "Did we really just do this?" I laughed. "Yeah, we did. We're here."

The emotion lasted exactly until immigration.

First hurdle: we went to the wrong counter. The officer was not happy about it — almost annoyed, he told us this wasn't the place, that we needed another section to sort out my wife's study permit, since she'd be studying at SAIT, and my work permit — me, the one who'd be working. Or rather: looking for work.

We found the right section. And there, the complete opposite: the second officer was kind, handled everything, issued both permits without any drama. But the whole mix-up had cost us — we were now late for our connecting flight. To top it off, we left through the wrong door.

Then the running started. The two of us, carry-ons in hand, racing across the airport in search of the right gate. We made it just in time — the plane was waiting only for us, and the agent was already calling our names over the speaker. We stepped on board out of breath, a little embarrassed, a little relieved, and sank into our seats.

Next stop: Calgary. Our new home.

When the plane finally landed, we waited for our bags — some had been checked — and met the person who would take us to our apartment.

Outside, it was about -10°C. (Looking back now, I think that wasn't even real cold. At the time, it felt absurd.) But honestly, in that moment the cold didn't even matter — we were too excited. We'd made it. We were here.

We got to the apartment, started unpacking, and got ready to rest after a long journey full of emotion. A journey that had begun, weeks earlier, with a knot in my stomach on the day I quit — and ended there, on the other side of the world, in a new home, in a new country, with snow outside the window.

The leap had happened. Now it was time to live it.

(And that's when the real challenges began — starting with English. But that's for the next chapter.)
